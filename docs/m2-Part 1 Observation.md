# Part 1 Observation



## Assessment of Your Categories

Your five categories are good. They cover the main sources of problem observation, and they're distinct enough to be useful. But there's one structural issue worth naming before we build on them.

**Categories 1, 2, and 4, 5 are sources.** They tell you *where to look*.
**Category 3 is a lens.** It tells you *how you see* — and it applies to all the other categories.

If you treat "skills and experiences" as just another source, you'll miss the fact that your experience changes what you notice in every other category. A logistics expert walking through a warehouse sees different problems than a designer walking through the same warehouse.

So the cleaner structure is:

**Sources (where to look):**

1. Personal problems and frustrations
2. Problems of people around you
3. Trends and external change
4. Broader market search and events

**Lens (how you see):**
5. Skills, experience, and lived experience

But — and this is important — the lens also produces its own problems. Your lived experience exposes you to problems that only people with that experience see. So it's both a lens and a source. That's fine. I'll treat it as a source with a note that it also colours everything else.

Two other small notes:

- **Categories 3 and 4 overlap.** Trends often show up as "problems of people around you" if you're embedded in the right communities. I'll keep them separate because the *method* is different, but flag the overlap.
- **One missing category worth considering:** *Problems solved elsewhere but not here.* Cross-pollination — a solution that exists in one industry, geography, or context that hasn't reached another. This is one of the most reliable sources of business ideas. I'll add it as a sub-category under "Broader market search."

Now let's build the observation stage.

---

## The Observation Stage, Structured by Source

For each source:

- **Why it matters** — what it gives you that others don't
- **What to ask** — the questions that surface problems
- **How to investigate** — concrete methods and places
- **What to capture** — what a good observation record looks like
- **Example** — what a real one sounds like
- **The trap** — what goes wrong if you're not careful

---

### Source 1 — Personal Problems and Frustrations

**Why it matters:** You are your first and best customer. Problems you personally experience are ones you understand deeply, can test cheaply, and have credibility on. You know the workarounds because you've built them. You know the cost because you've paid it. This is the highest-signal source for a first-time founder.

**What to ask:**

- What do I complain about repeatedly?
- What do I avoid, dread, or procrastinate on?
- What do I fix for myself over and over?
- What have I hacked together because nothing worked?
- What costs me time, money, or energy every week?
- What do I wish existed?
- What have I given up on solving?
- What do I do manually that feels like it should be automatic?
- What am I paying for that I resent?
- What do I search for repeatedly, hoping for a better answer?

**How to investigate:**

**Friction log (7 days).** Every time something annoys you, write it down. One line. Don't filter. At the end of the week, read it and look for patterns.

**Audit your own behaviour:**

- Search history — what do you keep searching for?
- Notes app — what do you keep noting?
- Browser bookmarks — what have you saved as "someday"?
- Camera roll — what have you photographed because you wanted to remember it?
- WhatsApp/Slack messages — what do you complain about?
- Bank statements — what are you paying for that you resent?

**Look at your workarounds:**

- What spreadsheet have you built to manage something?
- What manual process have you created?
- What have you automated for yourself?
- What have you given up on and just live with?

**Ask yourself:**

- "What would I pay someone to solve right now?"
- "What would I pay for if it existed?"
- "What do I do that I wish I didn't have to?"

**What to capture:**

- The problem in one sentence
- When it happens (trigger)
- What you do now (current workaround)
- What it costs you — time, money, emotion
- How often it happens
- How you'd know if it were solved

**Example:** *"Every Sunday I spend 45 minutes cross-referencing recipes, pantry inventory, and my family's dietary needs to plan the week's meals. I do this because I hate wasting food. I've tried three apps and abandoned all of them. I'd pay for something that just worked."*

**The trap:** Confusing "I want this" with "this is a problem worth solving." Also: problems that are unique to you and don't generalize. Test whether others have the same problem before treating it as an opportunity.

### Source 2 — Problems of People Around You

**Why it matters:** Expands your sample without leaving your reach. People you know will tell you the truth, and you can test cheaply with them. This is also where you'll find problems you can't see from your own vantage point — because you don't live their life.

**What to ask:**

- What do people come to me for help with?
- What do I hear people complain about repeatedly?
- What do I see people struggling with that they've accepted?
- What do people ask me to do for them?
- What are people around me spending money on badly?
- What workarounds do I see people using?
- Who is always stressed about the same thing?
- What do people say "someone should fix" or "why is this still like this"?
- What do people give up on?
- What are people embarrassed about not being able to do?

**How to investigate:**

**Listen for complaints, not ideas.** People will tell you their problems if you ask open questions. They won't say "someone should build X." They'll say "I hate that I have to Y."

**Ask open questions:**

- "What's the most annoying part of your week?"
- "What do you wish worked better?"
- "What's the thing you keep putting off because it's such a pain?"
- "What do you do that feels like a waste of time?"
- "What have you tried to fix and given up on?"

**Follow up:**

- "What do you do about it now?"
- "What does it cost you when it goes wrong?"
- "How often does this come up?"
- "Have you tried to solve it? Why did you stop?"

**Watch for workarounds:**

- The spreadsheet they maintain manually
- The WhatsApp group they use as a database
- The manual process they've accepted
- The thing they do "just in case"
- The thing they do twice because they don't trust the first result

**Expand your sample:**

- Talk to people in different contexts — family, work, community, hobby
- Talk to people in different roles — customers, suppliers, colleagues, neighbours
- Talk to people who do things differently than you

**What to capture:**

- Who has the problem (name, role, context)
- The problem in their words (quote if possible)
- What they currently do
- What it costs them
- How often it happens
- Whether they've tried to solve it
- How they'd know if it were solved

**Example:** *"Priya runs a small bakery. She spends 3 hours every night reconciling orders from WhatsApp, Instagram DMs, and her website. She's tried three tools and abandoned all of them because they don't handle multi-channel orders. She's resigned to doing it manually."*

**The trap:** Assuming people's problems are what you think they are. Also: polite complaints that don't reflect real pain. Ask "what do you do about it?" — if the answer is "nothing," it's often not urgent enough.

### Source 3 — Trends and External Change

**Why it matters:** Trends create new problems and make old problems newly urgent. Timing is a real advantage — being early to a wave can be worth more than being smart. Trends also give you a window into problems that will exist in 2–3 years, not just today.

**What to ask:**

- What technology, regulation, or behaviour is changing?
- What's becoming cheaper, faster, or more accessible?
- What's becoming more expensive, regulated, or scarce?
- What are people newly worried about?
- What's newly possible that wasn't 2 years ago?
- What's newly broken that wasn't 2 years ago?
- Who is being affected by these changes first?
- What old problems do these changes make worse?
- What new problems do these changes create?
- What problems are being created by the solution to another problem?

**How to investigate:**

**Technology trends:**

- **Product Hunt / Hacker News** — what's being built and talked about?
- **GitHub trending** — what are developers building?
- **AI/ML papers** — what's newly possible?
- **Hardware releases** — what new capabilities are available?

**Behavioural trends:**

- **Google Trends** — what's rising in search?
- **Exploding Topics / Glimpse** — emerging trends
- **Social media** — what are people newly obsessed with?
- **Cultural shifts** — what's becoming normal that wasn't?

**Regulatory trends:**

- **Government announcements** — what's being regulated?
- **Compliance requirements** — what's newly required?
- **Industry standards** — what's changing?

**Economic trends:**

- **Funding data** — what's getting invested in? (Crunchbase, Pitchbook)
- **Job boards** — what roles are suddenly in demand?
- **Pricing changes** — what's becoming more expensive?

**Demographic trends:**

- **Population shifts** — who's growing, who's shrinking?
- **Generational behaviour** — what do younger generations do differently?
- **Geographic shifts** — where are people moving?

**What to capture:**

- The trend
- What's changing
- Who is affected first
- What new problems it creates
- What old problems it makes worse
- How you'd know it's real vs hype
- What would have to be true for this to matter

**Example:** *"AI is making it possible to generate content at scale, but small businesses don't know how to use it without sounding generic. The new problem: how do you use AI without losing your voice? The old problem made worse: content overwhelm — now there's more content than ever, and less trust."*

**The trap:** Chasing trends instead of problems. Trends are context, not opportunities. Also: by the time a trend is obvious, it's often too late. Look for the second-order problems the trend creates.

### Source 4 — Broader Market Search and Events

**Why it matters:** This is deliberate, active exploration. You go looking for problems in places you don't normally go. It expands your sample beyond your immediate circle and exposes you to problems you'd never see otherwise. It's also where you find problems that have been solved elsewhere but not here.

**What to ask:**

- What problems are people in other industries struggling with?
- What problems are people in other geographies struggling with?
- What problems are showing up in communities I'm not part of?
- What problems are showing up in events, conferences, meetups?
- What problems are being funded, discussed, argued about?
- What problems are showing up in job postings?
- What problems do people ask about in forums?
- What problems are unanswered questions in Quora, Reddit, Stack Exchange?
- What problems are people trying to solve with bad tools?
- What problems have been solved in one context that haven't been solved in another?

**How to investigate:**

**Events and meetups:**

- Attend things outside your normal circle — industry events, community meetups, conferences
- Listen for what people complain about in hallway conversations
- Note what questions keep coming up in Q&A
- Talk to people you don't know — ask what they're struggling with

**Online communities:**

- Join communities you're not part of — lurk first, then ask
- Read the complaints, the questions, the "does anyone know how to..." posts
- Look at what gets upvoted, what gets argued about, what gets asked repeatedly

**Forums and Q&A:**

- **Reddit** — search for "how do I," "anyone else," "frustrated with," "alternative to"
- **Quora** — what questions keep getting asked?
- **Stack Exchange** — what are people trying to solve?
- **Industry forums** — what are practitioners struggling with?

**Job boards:**

- What roles are companies repeatedly hiring for?
- Those are problems they're paying to solve
- Look at the requirements — what skills are in demand?

**Freelance platforms:**

- **Upwork / Fiverr** — what are people outsourcing?
- That's a problem they can't solve internally
- Look at what's being requested repeatedly

**Reviews:**

- Read 1-star and 3-star reviews of products in adjacent spaces
- Complaints are problem statements
- Look for patterns — what do people consistently hate?

**Cross-pollination:**

- What works in one industry that doesn't exist in another?
- What works in one geography that hasn't reached another?
- What works for large companies that doesn't exist for small ones?
- What works for consumers that doesn't exist for businesses (or vice versa)?

**Search:**

- **Google** — "how do I," "alternative to," "frustrated with," "anyone else," "why is there no"
- **Google autocomplete** — reveals what people actually search
- **AnswerThePublic** — visualizes search questions

**What to capture:**

- The problem
- Where you found it
- Who has it
- What they do now
- Why it's unsolved
- Whether you can reach these people
- Whether you understand them

**Example:** *"In a freelance designer community, I keep seeing the same question: 'How do I handle scope creep without losing the client?' There are no good answers. Everyone has a hack. Some use contracts, some use boundaries, some just absorb the extra work. This is a real, recurring, unsolved problem — and freelancers are easy to reach."*

**The trap:** Going too broad. Also: finding problems you can't reach or understand. Just because a problem exists in a community you're not part of doesn't mean you can solve it. Proximity matters.

### Source 5 — Skills, Experience, and Lived Experience

**Why it matters:** This is your unfair advantage. Where you have deep experience, you see problems others can't see, and you have credibility to solve them. It's also a lens — it colours what you notice in every other category. A nurse sees different problems in a hospital than an administrator. A teacher sees different problems in a classroom than a parent.

**What to ask:**

- What have I done for years that others find hard?
- What industries do I know from the inside?
- What have I lived through that most people haven't?
- What do I know that I assume everyone knows?
- Where do I see inefficiency that others accept as normal?
- What have I built, fixed, organized, or led?
- What problems have I already solved for myself or others?
- What do I understand about a domain that outsiders don't?
- What's obvious to me that isn't obvious to others?
- What do people come to me for advice about?
- What failures or detours have taught me something useful?
- What's my unusual combination of skills and experiences?

**How to investigate:**

**Inventory your experience:**

- Work history — roles, industries, companies, projects
- Hobbies — what do you do for fun? What communities are you part of?
- Lived experience — what have you been through? What do you know firsthand?
- Side projects — what have you built, even if it failed?
- Failures — what went wrong? What did you learn?
- Unusual paths — what detours have you taken?

**For each, ask:**

- "What did I see there that outsiders wouldn't?"
- "What's the hardest part of this work?"
- "What do people in this world struggle with?"
- "What do they accept as normal that shouldn't be?"
- "What workarounds do they use?"
- "What would make this dramatically better?"

**Ask people in those contexts:**

- "What's the most annoying part of your work?"
- "What do you wish worked better?"
- "What do you do manually that feels like it should be automatic?"
- "What have you given up on?"

**Look for intersections:**

- What happens when you combine your skills?
- What problems sit at the intersection of your experience and a trend?
- What problems can you see because you've been in two worlds?

**What to capture:**

- Domain or experience
- The problem you see
- Why outsiders miss it
- What you understand that others don't
- How you'd approach solving it
- Whether you have credibility here

**Example:** *"I worked in cultural preservation for 10 years. I know how hard it is for small museums to digitize collections — not because of tech, but because of inconsistent metadata standards and no budget for catalogers. Most software assumes clean data. The real problem is data chaos. Outsiders build tools that don't survive contact with real museum data."*

**The trap:** Assuming your expertise translates to a market. Also: problems that are obvious to insiders but that outsiders don't care about. Test whether the problem matters to people who would pay.

### How to Use These Sources Together

Don't treat them as separate exercises. The best observations often come from the overlap:

- **Personal + Experience:** A problem you've lived through that you also have skills to solve.
- **People around you + Trends:** A problem your community has that's about to get worse because of a trend.
- **Market search + Experience:** A problem you found in research that you understand deeply because of your background.
- **Trends + Personal:** A new problem you're experiencing because of a change in the world.

**The strongest observations usually sit at the intersection of two or three sources.** An observation that's personal, grounded in your experience, and amplified by a trend is much stronger than one that's just from a trend.

## A Weekly Observation Practice

If you want to run this well, build a rhythm:

| Day           | Practice                                                           | Source            |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------- |
| **Monday**    | Review your friction log. Add one entry.                           | Personal          |
| **Tuesday**   | Ask one person about their week. Listen for complaints.            | People around you |
| **Wednesday** | Read one trend report, community, or forum. Note one problem.      | Trends / Market   |
| **Thursday**  | Look at your work history or a skill. What problem does it reveal? | Experience        |
| **Friday**    | Review the week. Look for patterns.                                | All               |
| **Weekend**   | One deliberate exploration — event, community, deep dive.          | Market / Events   |

At the end of two weeks, you should have 20–30 raw observations. Then you qualify them.

---

## What a Good Observation Record Looks Like

For each observation, capture:

- **The problem** — in one sentence
- **Who has it** — specific person or group
- **Source** — where you noticed it (personal, people, trend, market, experience)
- **Evidence** — what you saw, heard, or read
- **Current workaround** — what they do now
- **Cost** — time, money, emotion
- **Frequency** — how often it happens
- **Your connection** — why you noticed it, why you care
- **Initial flags** — real? care? can I solve?

That last line is where the next stage begins.

---

## The One Thing to Remember

Observation is not passive. It's a skill you build by looking with intention, from multiple angles, over time. The five sources are lenses. Use them all. Look through each one deliberately.

The goal isn't to find *an* idea. It's to build a **practice of noticing** — so that when a real problem shows up, you see it clearly, and you have enough evidence to know it's worth your time.
